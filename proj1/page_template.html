Approach

Each plate contains blue, green, and red exposures stacked vertically. I split the plate into equal thirds, convert the pixels to floats, and find translations that align the channels before combining them into RGB.

Single-scale: try every shift within ±15 pixels in each direction (961 candidates), then keep the best score. I exclude 10% of each edge plus space for all candidate shifts, so borders and wrapped pixels do not affect the score.

Pyramid: halve the images with anti-aliasing until the largest dimension is at most 400 pixels. Align the smallest pair, double the offset at each larger level, and refine within ±2 pixels. All images use these same settings.

  • L2: √Σ(A − B)². Minimize pixel differences.
  • NCC: Σ(A′B′) / (‖A′‖2 ‖B′‖2), where A′ = A − mean(A). Maximize correlation after removing each image's mean and normalizing its magnitude.

Green is aligned to blue. Red is aligned to green, then the two shifts are added to place red in blue's coordinates. This avoids relying on a direct red-to-blue brightness match, which failed on Emir.

Alignment and remaining artifacts

Emir: matching red directly to blue produced doubled facial features. Matching red to green instead changes the final NCC red offset from (45, 78) to @@EMIR_RED_OFFSET@@ and visibly improves the face and clothing. The same channel-matching rule is used for every image.

Borders: the crop is used only for scoring. Saved images retain the scan borders and pixels wrapped by shifting, so colored edge bands are expected. Cropping the final image would remove those bands, but would not fix misaligned details inside the scene.

Limitations: small fringes remain on some fine edges, people, foliage, and water. A single translation cannot correct local motion or differences in scale and rotation. Visual inspection is useful, but without ground-truth offsets it does not establish perfect alignment.
